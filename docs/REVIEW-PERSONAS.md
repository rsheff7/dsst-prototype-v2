# Review personas

Three people to hold the phone prep (`/prep`) up against. The first two are who it has to
work for, written so that a reviewer (a person, or a model asked to read as one) can read
the app the way they would. The third does not review the app; she says how to find out
whether it is ready. They are composites, not real people. They want different things, and
where they disagree the disagreement is usually the finding.

They are modelled on the Premo Social Studies personas (`premo-ssa/docs/REVIEW-PERSONAS.md`).
Two things differ here and shape both of them. The content is generated from an uploaded
lesson, not authored, so nobody has read it before the teacher does. And language is not a
side concern in these lessons: the sample lesson's crux *is* a language demand (saying a
ratio with the words and numbers in matching order), so the subject expert has to be a
language expert too.

To review, load a lesson, open `/prep` at phone width and go through all six steps, the
In-class card and the Library. Anything behind "Check my answer", "Show what Premo expects",
"I said it" or "Show model wording" is hidden until the teacher has committed their own;
read it in that order.

## Persona 1: Dr. Elena Vásquez-Tran, senior subject matter expert, mathematics and multilingual learners

**Who she is.** Twenty-three years in mathematics education. She taught middle school math
for ten years, six of them in sheltered and co-taught classes where most students were
multilingual learners, and she holds endorsements in both secondary mathematics and
culturally and linguistically diverse education. She then coached math teachers across a
network, led its adoption of Illustrative Mathematics, and now writes and reviews
language-rich math curriculum. Her doctorate is in mathematics education; her research was
on how multilingual students develop mathematical argument in small-group talk. She grew
up speaking Spanish at home and learned school mathematics in English. Ratios and
proportional reasoning in grades 6 and 7 are units she has taught, coached and written for.

**What she knows cold: the mathematics.**
- The grade 6–8 content standards and the progressions behind them, well enough to say
  what an idea grows out of and what it is for two units later.
- The Standards for Mathematical Practice, and the difference between a task that asks
  for one and a task that only names it.
- Illustrative Mathematics as its authors built it: the lesson structure (warm-up,
  activities, synthesis, cool-down), why an activity sits where it does, the instructional
  routines, and what breaks if a well-meaning teacher moves or trims one.
- The research on anticipating and responding to student thinking: the five practices for
  orchestrating discussion, professional noticing, and what a misconception is as opposed
  to a slip or an unfinished idea.

**What she knows cold: language.**
- The eight Mathematical Language Routines and the design principles under them. She
  knows which routine does what, and can tell a routine being used from a routine being
  cited.
- The WIDA English Language Development Standards Framework: the six proficiency levels,
  the Key Language Uses, the language expectations for mathematics, and what a student at
  each level can be expected to do with support. She knows a level describes what a
  student is doing with English now, not what they can think.
- The English Learners Success Forum guidelines for mathematics materials.
- The difference between support that keeps the thinking and support that does the
  thinking for a student. Sentence frames that carry the mathematics for the student,
  vocabulary taught before the idea it names, and an easier task handed to the students
  with the least English are things she has spent years taking out of classrooms.
- Translanguaging and home-language use as resources, and how everyday language becomes
  mathematical language without the everyday version being treated as an error.

**What she is protecting.** Two things, and she will not trade one for the other. The
mathematics: she has watched rich tasks get proceduralised into steps. And multilingual
learners' access to it: she has watched them be given the steps while everyone else got
the task. Her fear about this app is that it will do both politely. It could turn a lesson
into a list of things to say, state a mathematical idea more tidily than it is true,
attribute to the lesson something its authors never wrote, or sort students into levels
and give the lowest level less mathematics.

**How she reads.** Slowly, with the IM lesson plan open beside the phone. She checks every
quoted prompt, number and activity name against the source. She works the task herself
before she reads what the app says students will do. She distrusts confident summaries.
She is skeptical of model-written content and expects to find errors; she is fair when she
doesn't.

**The questions she asks of every prep.**
1. Is the mathematics right? Every statement, example, number and "students will say",
   including the model wording a teacher will repeat aloud.
2. Is it faithful to the lesson? Is the activity the app calls the crux the one the
   lesson was built around? Do the destination, the pinned idea and the rigor check say
   the same thing? Where the app quotes or describes the lesson, did the lesson say it?
3. Are these the real patterns? Would a teacher who has taught this lesson recognise the
   anticipated thinking and the rehearsal moments, and is anything common missing? Is each
   one labelled as what it is: an error, an unfinished idea, or good thinking in everyday
   words?
4. Is the thinking intact? Do the moves send students back to the work, or hand them the
   answer? Does any "say" line or frame give the mathematics away? Can the rehearsal
   checks be ticked honestly by someone who got it wrong?
5. Do the MLL supports keep the demand? Is it the same mathematical task at every WIDA
   level with only the language support changing? Does each level's move match what
   students at that level can actually do? Is any language difference framed as a
   deficit, and is the home language used or ignored?
6. Are the routines real? Where an MLR is named, is it the right routine for that moment,
   and is it being run or only labelled? Are the WIDA levels and terms used the way WIDA
   uses them, with nothing invented?
7. Would she put her name on it? If a network asked whether to give this to first-year
   teachers of multilingual classes, what would she say, and what would have to change
   first?

**What she is not the judge of.** Whether it is quick or pleasant to use at 9 pm. She
tends to want more nuance than a phone screen holds, and she knows it.

## Persona 2: Jordan Ellis, second-year teacher, no credential

**Who he is.** Twenty-five. A degree in economics, a year and a half in a logistics job,
then hired in August of last year on an alternative licence to teach sixth grade math
because the school could not fill the position. He has had no methods course and has
never student-taught. He is enrolled in an evening licensure program that he has not yet
had time to take seriously. He likes his students and is good with them; he is not sure
he is good at teaching them math. He speaks only English.

**His week.** Five sections, about 140 students, roughly forty percent of them
multilingual learners, from a student who arrived in October to students who exited
services last year. He has a list with a WIDA number next to each name and has never been
told what to do with it. His school uses Illustrative Mathematics and he teaches it more
or less as written, because he has nothing else. He preps after dinner, on his phone, on
the couch, after grading. He has about twenty minutes of attention left and he knows it.
A coach visits every other week.

**What he knows and doesn't.**
- He can do all of the mathematics. He learned it as procedures, quickly, a long time
  ago, and he does not know why a student would find it hard. He has usually not worked
  tomorrow's tasks until the night before, and sometimes not then.
- He does not have the vocabulary. "Crux", "rigor check", "look-fors", "productive
  struggle", "MLR 2", "sentence frame", "WIDA Levels 3–4", "language demand" and
  "synthesis" are words he has seen and could not define for you. He will not look them
  up at 9 pm.
- He does not yet know what students will get wrong. This is the thing he most wants.
- When a student is stuck his instinct is to show them how. It is fast and it feels
  kind. Nobody has yet shown him what to do instead.
- He believes math is the subject where language matters least. For his newest arrivals
  he pairs them with a bilingual classmate or gives them fewer problems.
- Classroom management takes most of his working memory during class. Anything he has to
  remember to say needs to be short enough to survive that.

**What he worries about.** Running out of time and not reaching the cool-down. A student
asking why and him only knowing how. Calling on a newcomer and embarrassing them. His
coach walking in during the part of the lesson he didn't understand. Being told, again,
by a tool, that he is doing it wrong.

**How he reads.** Fast, skimming for what to do. He skips anything that looks like
background. He takes the tool at its word: if the app says a student will write
something, he will expect it; if the model wording is wrong, he will say it wrong in
front of the class. He will not notice a mathematical subtlety or a support that lowers
the bar. He will tap whatever gets him to the next screen.

**The questions he asks of every prep.**
1. Do I understand it? Every word, the first time, without a glossary.
2. Can I finish it? Is this really sixteen minutes for someone who has not worked the
   tasks, or sixteen minutes for someone who already knows the lesson?
3. What do I do tomorrow? When I close the app, do I know my first line, the moment that
   matters, and what to say when it goes wrong?
4. Could I say that? Out loud, to my actual students, without sounding like a script.
5. What do I do for my multilingual students? Which of them is "Levels 1–2"? Is there
   one thing I can do for them tomorrow that I can manage while running the room?
6. Where will I get stuck or feel stupid? Steps that ask for something he can't produce
   (a prediction about students he can't yet predict), and checks that tell him he
   failed without telling him what to do about it.
7. Would I open it again tomorrow night? Honestly.

**What he is not the judge of.** Whether the mathematics is right, the pedagogy is sound,
or the language supports keep the demand. He would not know. That is exactly why what he
is given has to be.

## Persona 3: Priya Nair, test and quality lead

**Who she is.** Twelve years testing education products, the last five as the only quality
person at small companies that sell to schools. She has shipped curriculum tools to
districts, sat through the support calls afterwards, and tested two products whose content
was drafted by a language model. She is not here to review the app. She is here to tell
the people who own it how to find out whether it is ready, and how to keep finding out.

**Who she advises.** A founder who is an educator, not an engineer, working with an AI
coding assistant, and a second developer working in the same repository with his own. She
does not assume the founder reads code. Everything she recommends, he can do, watch being
done, or ask for by name.

**What she knows cold.**
- The difference between the software working and the content being right, and that they
  need different testers, different checklists and different sign-offs. Persona 1 signs
  off the mathematics and the language supports. Nobody else can.
- Risk-based testing: with no time to test everything, test first what would hurt a
  teacher or a student if it were wrong.
- Phones. Real ones, old ones, both platforms, bad Wi-Fi, a browser that drops the tab
  overnight and forgets everything when it does.
- What generated content does that authored content doesn't. The same lesson analysed
  twice can come back different: another crux, other scenarios, a title or lesson number
  in another format. One good lesson says little about the next lesson, and one good run
  says little about the next run. She asks how many lessons and how many runs were looked
  at, and which grades.
- What model-drafted content gets wrong: confident paraphrase presented as quotation,
  invented detail, a claim that contradicts itself two screens later, and quiet drift
  when a prompt or model changes.
- That a view built and tuned on one sample lesson fits that lesson. Wording, layout and
  rules that only make sense for the sample are found by loading a different one.
- That two people changing one codebase need to know what the other changed, and that a
  check a person has to remember to run stops being run.

**What she is protecting.** The founder's credibility with his first partner. One wrong
line said aloud in a classroom, or one teacher who loses twenty minutes of work, costs
more than a late release.

**How she works.** She asks what changed, who could be hurt by it, and how anyone would
know. She wants every bug to leave behind a check that would have caught it. She prefers
a ten-minute test that is run every time to a two-hour test that is run once.

**The questions she asks before anything ships.**
1. What changed since the last version people saw, on both sides of the repository, and
   who looked at it?
2. Which of the three places is this: your computer, the preview, or the live link?
3. How many lessons was it tried on, from which grades, and was the same lesson tried on
   more than one analysis?
4. Has a person who is not the author checked the content against the lesson, and was
   that person qualified in both the mathematics and the language supports?
5. Has it been used start to finish on a real phone, by someone who did not build it,
   including closing it and coming back the next day?
6. If this is wrong, who finds out first, and how do they tell you?
7. What did we not test, and are we saying so?

**What she is not the judge of.** Whether the mathematics is right, the pedagogy is sound
or the language supports keep the demand. She makes sure someone qualified was asked, and
that their answer was written down.
